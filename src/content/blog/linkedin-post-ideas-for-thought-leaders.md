Good LinkedIn post ideas come from four sources: your work, your disagreements, your questions, and your data. Most thought leaders run dry because they only draw from one of those wells, usually "my work," and it turns into a highlight reel. Below is an idea bank organized by what each post is actually for, not just what it looks like.

## Why most thought leaders run out of post ideas

The common failure mode is treating LinkedIn like a resume feed: announcements, congratulations, and recycled quotes. That content gets polite engagement and builds nothing. A post idea only works if it does one of three jobs: it teaches something specific, it takes a position someone can argue with, or it documents evidence of your thinking in progress. If a draft doesn't do one of those three things, it's filler, and audiences can tell within the first line.

## LinkedIn post ideas by strategic purpose

### Ideas that build authority

- **The framework post.** Name a process you use (a 4-step method, a 3-question filter) and walk through it with one real example. Frameworks get saved and shared because they're reusable.
- **The mistake breakdown.** Describe a specific error you made or saw a client make, what it cost, and the fix. Specificity beats polish here.
- **The counterintuitive result.** "Everyone assumes X. Our data (or three years of client work) says Y." This works only if you can back it with a number or a named example.
- **The glossary post.** Define a term your industry uses loosely, then give your own working definition. Useful for SEO too, since these get searched directly.

### Ideas that spark conversation

- **The unpopular opinion.** A real position, not a manufactured one. If you wouldn't say it in a room of peers, don't post it.
- **The this-or-that.** Two approaches, your honest take on when each applies. Avoid false binaries; readers spot them fast.
- **The open question.** Post a genuine unresolved question from your work. Ask readers how they'd solve it. This performs well because it invites comments, but only if you actually engage with the replies.
- **The prediction with a date.** "By [year], I think X will be standard." Predictions age publicly, which is exactly why they build credibility when you're right and credibility when you're honest about being wrong.

### Ideas that show proof of work

- **The behind-the-scenes.** A screenshot of your research process, a rough outline, a client call takeaway (anonymized). Raw beats refined for this format.
- **The before-and-after.** A specific problem, the intervention, the measured result. Numbers matter more than adjectives.
- **The reading log.** What you read this month and the one idea from each that changed how you work. Signals depth without requiring original research every time.
- **The client question, answered publicly.** Take a question you got in a DM or a call, strip identifying details, and answer it as a post. It's efficient content and it proves you're actually doing the work.

> A post idea only earns a slot in your calendar if it teaches, argues, or proves something. Everything else is a placeholder.

## What makes a LinkedIn post idea actually work

An idea is only as good as its specificity. "Share thoughts on leadership" is not an idea, it's a category. "Explain why the 1-on-1 meeting format most managers use wastes the first 10 minutes, and what to do instead" is an idea. The difference is a claim you can defend and a reader can act on.

Three filters worth applying before you draft:

- **Can someone screenshot the middle three lines and understand the point?** If not, it's buried.
- **Does it contain one number, one name, or one concrete outcome?** This is the same standard we hold ourselves to in client work: no vague claims, no unverifiable gestures at expertise.
- **Would you defend this in a comment thread with a skeptic?** If the answer is no, soften the claim or drop the post.

## How often should thought leaders post on LinkedIn?

Three to five times a week is a reasonable range for most experts building an audience, though consistency over months matters more than frequency in any single week. A thought leader posting twice a week for a year, with each post carrying a real idea, will outperform someone posting daily for six weeks and then going quiet. LinkedIn's algorithm rewards accounts it can predict, and predictability comes from a schedule you can actually sustain, not from posting volume alone.

### A simple weekly rhythm

- **Monday:** a framework or teaching post (the week's highest-effort piece)
- **Wednesday:** an opinion or open question (lower lift, higher engagement)
- **Friday:** a proof-of-work post (client insight, reading log, or result)

This rhythm gives you one idea from each category without requiring three heavy research sessions a week.

## Turning one idea into a week of content

A single strong idea, an original framework, a client result, a contrarian take, can usually be split across three or four formats: a full post, a shorter carousel breaking down the same point visually, a quote card pulling the sharpest line, and a follow-up comment-bait question a few days later. ## FAQ

### How many LinkedIn post ideas should I keep in a content bank?
Aim for at least 20 to 30 at any time, organized by the three categories above (teach, argue, prove). This gives you roughly two months of material without repeating an angle.

### Do LinkedIn post ideas need data to work?
Not always, but the strongest ones carry a number, a named example, or a specific outcome. A vague opinion post performs worse than the same opinion backed by one data point or one client story.

### Should thought leaders repost old LinkedIn content?
Yes, with a new framing. Reposting a strong idea after 3 to 6 months, updated with a new example or result, usually performs as well as the original because most of your audience never saw it the first time.
