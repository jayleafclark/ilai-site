How to write a newsletter people open starts with one decision made before you type a word: pick a single reader and a single reason they'd stop scrolling for you. Every subject line, every subhead, every send time follows from that choice. Skip it and you get a well-written email nobody opens.

## Why most newsletters go unread

The average newsletter open rate across industries sits between 20% and 30%, according to Mailchimp's 2023 benchmark data. That means most subscribers never see what you wrote. The usual cause isn't bad writing. It's a subject line that promises nothing specific, a body that takes three paragraphs to say something, and a sending pattern so irregular that readers forget they subscribed.

Fix the structure and the writing gets easier, because you're no longer trying to be clever. You're trying to be useful on a schedule.

## How to write a newsletter: the format that works

Use this five-part shape for almost any expert newsletter:

- **Subject line (under 50 characters).** State the specific value, not a tease. "3 client emails that stopped no-shows" beats "You won't believe this."
- **One-line opener.** Answer the subject line immediately. Readers decide in the first sentence whether to keep going.
- **The body (200-400 words).** One idea, explained with a number, an example, or a short story. Not three ideas loosely connected.
- **One clear next step.** A link, a question, a reply prompt. Pick one.
- **A closing line with personality.** A sign-off that sounds like a person, not a brand template.

This format works because it matches how people actually read email: fast, on a phone, looking for a reason to stay past the first line.

### Subject lines that earn the open

- Use numbers: "5 questions I ask before every strategy call"
- Name the outcome: "How I cut editing time by 40%"
- Ask a real question your reader has: "Why did my last launch flop?"
- Avoid vague curiosity bait. "This changed everything" tests worse than a plain, specific line in almost every A/B test we've reviewed.

### Structure the body around one idea

Pick a single takeaway and build the whole email around it. If you have three good ideas, that's three newsletters, not one crowded one. Readers remember one thing per email. Give them one thing worth remembering.

## What should a newsletter actually say?

A newsletter should say something a reader can use in the next 24 hours. That's the test. If the content only makes sense in the abstract, it gets skimmed and archived. If it gives a reader a specific move (a script, a checklist, a number to try), it gets saved.

Three content types perform consistently well for expert and thought-leader newsletters:

1. **The lesson.** What you learned this week and the specific change you made because of it.
2. **The breakdown.** One example (a post, a call, a client situation, described without names) taken apart piece by piece.
3. **The list.** Three to five short, concrete items on one theme, each one sentence long.

> A newsletter earns its next open with the last one it sent, not the promise of the one after that.

## How often should you send a newsletter?

Weekly is the sweet spot for most expert newsletters. It's frequent enough to stay in a reader's habit and infrequent enough that you can hit a real standard each time. Biweekly works if your content needs more research per issue. Monthly usually fails, not from lack of quality, but from lack of memory. Readers forget why they subscribed and start marking future issues as spam.

Whatever cadence you choose, keep the send day and time fixed. Consistency trains the open, more than any single subject line trick.

## Editing pass: cut before you publish

Before sending, run every draft through three cuts:

- **Cut the first paragraph if it's a warm-up.** Start where the value starts.
- **Cut adjectives that don't carry information.** "Amazing results" tells a reader nothing. "37% more replies" does.
- **Cut any sentence that repeats what the subject line already said.** Redundancy reads as filler, and filler is the fastest way to lose a subscriber's attention for good.

Read the draft out loud once. If you stumble on a sentence, a reader will stumble on it too, and stumbling is where they close the tab.

## FAQ

### How long should a newsletter be?
Most expert newsletters work best between 300 and 500 words. Long enough for one full idea, short enough to finish on a coffee break.

### How do I grow a newsletter list without paid ads?
Consistent value plus a clear signup prompt on every piece of content you publish (podcast show notes, social bios, articles) builds a list steadily. Referral prompts inside the newsletter itself add a second growth channel at no extra cost.

### Should a newsletter match my other content?
Yes. Tone and topics should echo what you publish elsewhere, but the format should stay email-native: shorter, more direct, and built around one idea rather than several.
